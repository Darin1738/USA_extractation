# PAN-AFRICAN SYMBOLISM — SOURCED BRIEF

Research brief for the **background collage + presentation** of *The Name Changes. The Function Does Not.* (HIST 350, Group 4). Every claim below has a verifiable source at the end (`[#]` = Sources list). Written 21 Oct 2026.

> Purpose: the collage should use symbols because of **what they mean to the people who carry them** — not as decoration. This document says what each means, who it belongs to, and where to verify it.

---

## 1. Why symbolism matters here

Marcus Garvey understood that an oppressed people needs **its own images**, arguing: *"Show me the race or the nation without a flag, and I will show you a race of people without any pride."* [1][2] Symbols were not ornament for the UNIA — they were tools of **psycho-cultural repair and unity**, designed "to promote pride and unity." [3] That is the license for this board's design: the collage is carrying the same work the panels are.

- **Pan-Africanism** = the movement to unite Black people of Africa and the Diaspora and to secure independence and self-determination. It runs through a series of international **Pan-African Congresses**: London 1900 (W.E.B. Du Bois, Henry Sylvester Williams), Paris 1919, London/Paris/Brussels 1921, Lisbon/London 1923, New York 1927, Manchester 1945. Manchester 1945 is the hinge: it included **Kwame Nkrumah** (later president of Ghana) and **Jomo Kenyatta** (later president of Kenya) and shifted from petitioning to demanding **immediate liberation**. [4][5][6]

## 2. The pan-African colours — red, black, green

The **Pan-African flag** (also UNIA flag, Marcus Garvey flag, Black Liberation flag) is three equal horizontal bands — red, black, green — adopted by the UNIA in **1920**, in part as an answer to the racist song *"Every Race Has a Flag but the Coon."* [1][2][7]

Standard meanings:

| Colour | Meaning | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Red** | the blood that unites all people of Black African ancestry, and the blood shed for liberation | [1][7] |
| **Black** | Black people, whose existence as a nation — though not a nation-state — is affirmed by the flag | [1][7] |
| **Green** | the abundant natural wealth of Africa / the land | [1][7] |

Garvey's own emphasis, as later recalled in Steel Pulse's *Rally Round*: *"Red for the blood that flowed like a river… Green for the land, Africa… Yellow [gold] for the gold that they stole."* [8]

**Design takeaway:** red = sacrifice/life, black = the people, green = the land. This is why red and green can carry a **resistance** layer over a **black** ground: red/green on black is literally *the people and their struggle on the people*. **Gold** is used in this project as the stolen wealth/dignity motif — note it is *not* part of the 1920 Garvey tricolour (black nationalism groups like the African National Congress use black/green/gold), so gold here is used as an accent of value, not as a claim about the flag. [1][8][9]

## 3. Adinkra — Akan symbols of Ghana (proverbs as images)

**Adinkra** are visual symbols from the **Akan** people of Ghana, representing concepts, proverbs and aphorisms; they date to the 1700s or earlier and were originally stamped on cloth worn by royalty at funerals and ceremonies. [10][11][12] Akan proverbs are the template: *"Cloth as Metaphor."* [13] Adinkra is **not** a fixed, uncontested set — meanings vary and are debated — so cite the symbol and its proverb rather than asserting one universal meaning. [10][14]

Symbols relevant to *this* subject (freedom, resistance, memory, endurance):

| Symbol | Meaning | Why it fits this board |
|---|---|---|
| **Sankofa** ("go back and get it") | wisdom of learning from the past to build the future; proverb *"Se wo were firi na wosan kofa a, yenkyiri"* — it is not taboo to go back for what you forgot | The whole project is a Sankofa act: recovering the through-line from slavery to the present. Most used Adinkra symbol **beyond** Ghana, especially among African Americans seeking roots. [11][12] |
| **Gye Nyame** ("except God") | omnipotence/supremacy of God | Deep spirituality of the people; most popular Adinkra symbol in Ghana. [11] |
| **Akoben** ("war horn") | vigilance, wariness; a **call to arms / willingness to take action** | Revolution & resistance register. [15][16] |
| **Akofena** ("sword of war") | courage, valor | Resistance. [15] |
| **Aya** (fern) | *"I am not afraid of you"* — **defiance** | Freedom in any form. [16] |
| **Nkonsonkonso** | human relations: *"those who share blood relations never break apart"* | Diaspora unity. [16] |
| **Hye Wo Nhye** ("that which cannot be burnt") | imperishability, endurance | Survival of the people. [15] |
| **Nyame Dua** ("tree of God") | God's protection and presence | Memory/faith. [15] |
| **Mframadan** ("wind-resistant house") | fortitude, preparedness | Endurance under attack. [15] |
| **Adinkrahene** ("chief of adinkra symbols") | greatness, charisma, leadership | Leadership/unity. [15] |

## 4. Kente — woven identity

**Kente** is handwoven cloth from Ghana; the word comes from Akan *kenten* = "basket." Historically reserved for **royalty and sacred occasions**, worn today at graduations, weddings and diasporic ceremonies as a **Pan-African emblem of identity**. Kente and Adinkra are often paired — both rooted in Akan philosophy, both "visual languages." [17] **Kwame Nkrumah** used kente to embody Pan-African pride. [14] The board uses a **kente strip** as a woven band of colour and identity surviving the crossing.

## 5. The raised fist — the Black Power salute

The **clenched/raised fist** is an ancient gesture (Assyrian art associates the fist with strength) that became a **modern symbol of defiance and solidarity** through labour, leftist and civil-rights movements. [18] Its defining Black-liberation image:

- **16 October 1968, Mexico City Olympics** — Tommie Smith (gold) and John Carlos (bronze) raised **black-gloved fists** on the podium during the U.S. anthem, organized through the **Olympic Project for Human Rights** (Harry Edwards). Smith wore a black scarf (Black pride); Carlos wore beads "for those individuals that were lynched, or killed and that no-one said a prayer for." They stood in **black socks, no shoes**, to protest Black poverty. Smith later called it *"a cry for freedom and for human rights."* Both were suspended; the image became one of the most iconic of the 20th century and was revived by athletes such as Colin Kaepernick. [19][20][21][22]

The fist here is not generic radicalism — it is **specifically the Black freedom struggle's** gesture for dignity and human rights.

## 6. Other symbols used, and their grounding

- **Dove** — peace. Used in a **tension** with barbed wire/war so the board does not romanticize peace; peace was fought for.
- **Broken chain / shackles** — the Smithsonian's NMAAHC holds actual **iron shackles** (pre-1860) in the Liljenquist collection, a direct material witness to enslavement; the broken chain reads as *freedom seized, not granted*. [23]
- **Torch** — carried light / intergenerational memory.
- **Megaphone** — organized voice, protest.
- **Poppy** — remembrance; ties the board's "memory" panel to the obligation to remember the dead (and to the Tulsa panel).
- **Barbed wire** — enclosure/confinement; the architecture of control (prison, plantation, border).
- **Thread (red)** — the board's through-line: profit → punishment → prediction.
- **Globe (Africa-centred)** — Diaspora reach: the same pattern across the hemisphere (Brazil, Argentina, U.S.).

## 7. Tulsa — why "Remember Tulsa" is on the board

The **Tulsa Race Massacre** (31 May–1 June 1921): white mobs attacked the Greenwood district — "**Black Wall Street**," so named because Booker T. Washington had nicknamed it "Negro Wall Street." Homes, businesses, a hospital, churches and a library were looted and burned. Estimates: **around 10,000 people left homeless and as many as 300 dead**, with many more missing/wounded; **exact statistics are unknown**. [24][25] Photographs survive in the public domain (LoC and NMAAHC/CC0). [24][26] This grounds the "gold/wealth destroyed" and remembrance themes.

---

## 8. Honesty notes (things not to overstate)

- Adinkra meanings are **contested/fluid**; present them as Akan proverbs, not fixed doctrine. [10][14]
- The 1920 Garvey flag is **red/black/green only**; **gold is not one of its colours.** Don't claim otherwise. [1][7]
- The raised fist means different things to different movements; here we anchor it to the **1968 Black Power salute** and its own stated meaning. [19][21]
- Tulsa casualty figures are **approximate** (36 by some official counts vs. 100–300+ by others); say "as many as 300" and "estimates." [24]
