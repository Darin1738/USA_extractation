## P2 — 1619–1865: Slavery & Racial Order

Slavery racialized labor. The exploitation of free labor was a large economic incentive for slavery, paving the way for slaves being deemed property, further emboldening their captives to act with great cruelty and brutality. Ultimately denying personhood and dehumanizing enslaved people. To continue this economic engine, rationales from biblical to physiological were created to further dehumanize Black individuals and justify the cruelties of slavery. ~12.5 million Africans were forced into the Atlantic slave trade, with only about 10.7 million of them disembarking alive in the Americas. The U.S. system was built to extract labor from Black bodies and deny their humanity — long before the abolition of slavery.

Now, the honest counterpoint: only a tiny percentage of the Africans taken in the Atlantic trade ever landed in North America.

**[Q] "If most enslaved Africans went elsewhere, why focus on the U.S.?"**

Because what was built here wasn't only numbers — it was a racial *order*, the blueprint that everything after this panel runs on.

## P4 — 1865–66: Black Codes & Vagrancy

With the introduction of black codes, the law criminalized unemployment, loitering, and migration. This is simply the re-criminalization of freedom — Black people roaming "free" were presumed illegal. By leaving criminals out of the clause banning involuntary servitude, the only thing left to do was ensure Black people were the majority of criminals, and the economic engine simply takes new form. Any infraction, even just an unpaid fine, rolls straight into forced labor.

From a newspaper record, one person commented on the current state of affairs: *"African Americans and justice-involved individuals were often convicted of petty crimes, like walking on the grass, vagrancy, and stealing food... Even those who were declared innocent in the courts were often placed in this system when they could not pay their court fees."*

And notice — that same machine is running today. Fines, fees, cash bail: an unpaid balance still becomes somebody's labor.

**[Q] "Can you be free if your freedom is a crime?"**

**[Q] "What happens when an unpaid fine becomes labor?"**

## P5 — 1868–1940s: Convict Leasing ("slavery by another name")

States would then lease convicts to all types of projects and institutions — plantations, railroads, mines. Meaning not only was the state profiting from Black bodies, but so were the foundations of emerging American industry. Douglas Blackmon's research exposed exactly how it worked: Southern governments arrested Black Americans, often on fabricated or minor charges, and "leased" their bodies as forced labor to private corporations, plantations, and railroads.

Convict leasing was so lucrative it has been estimated at roughly **73% of Alabama's state revenue** in the late 1800s (approximate, no exact figure survives); by 1900, Black people were a large majority of Southern convicts.

**[Q] "Who got paid?"**

The state, and industry. Never the person in the chain gang.

**[Q] "What is the difference between owning a person and renting their punishment?"**

The plantation wasn't destroyed. It was rebuilt behind prison walls — and it turned a profit.

## P6 — 1890s–1960s: Jim Crow Policing & Lynching

At this point the culmination of the policing of Black bodies — from black codes, to propaganda in media portraying Blackness as wicked, savage, unintelligent, ultimately less than human, and the criminalization of Black people — resulted in Jim Crow and lynching mobs. Central to it was the manufactured myth of the "Black rapist," which gave cover to racial terror and racialized policing. The rationales of the past were only further spread and reinforced, justifying the lynching of women, men, and children on nothing more than a criminal accusation. On no basis, no fair trial, life is taken.

In the EJI documents covering 1877–1950, there are **~4,400 racial-terror lynchings** of Black people recorded. And a reminder from Ida B. Wells and the records of the time: the accusation was the evidence. Mississippi congressman Thomas Sisson declared he would rather kill every Black person in the world than have just one white girl raped by a Black man — that is a lie becoming policy out loud.

## P7 — 1971–Present: War on Drugs → Mass Incarceration

This control takes another form in Nixon's presidency as he declared war on drugs. A noble cause on the surface — but disproportionately, the war on drugs was found fought in communities of color through selective enforcement. Then the 1986 Anti-Drug Abuse Act with its 100:1 crack-to-cocaine disparity, the 1994 Crime Bill, mandatory minimums.

A quote attributed to Nixon adviser John Ehrlichman, from a 1994 interview — and I'll flag it: it's widely cited but its attribution is disputed, so take it as reported, not proven: *"The Nixon campaign in 1968, and the Nixon White House after that, had two enemies: the antiwar left and Black people… We knew we couldn't make it illegal to be either against the war or Black, but by getting the public to associate the hippies with marijuana and Blacks with heroin, and then criminalizing both heavily, we could disrupt those communities… Did we know we were lying about the drugs? Of course we did."*

While only ~5% of the world's population resides in the USA, we hold roughly a quarter of the world's prisoners. Mass incarceration is truly an understatement. In the same data, while Black people were ~13% of the U.S. population, they made up 37% of U.S. prisoners. Nixon's war on drugs was a framework within the U.S. system that enabled mass incarceration both structurally and politically. Through this, once again but only stronger, **"Law & Order" became the new racial control.**

## P8 — 2000s–Present: Plantation → Algorithm

Through the centuries there has been manufactured and perpetuated discrimination and cruelty against Black bodies. Woven into the fabric of the freedom the United States is built on, many stereotypes and preconceived notions have been adopted into algorithms and systems of automation that, at scale, only exacerbate the problem.

Concretely: **COMPAS** — a risk-score software used in sentencing and parole that decides how likely a court is told a defendant is to reoffend. A study by ProPublica in **2016** found Black defendants were flagged high-risk at **2x the rate** of white defendants. Also, in **2019**, NIST found that face-recognition false positives were anywhere from **10 to 100x more likely** for Black and Asian faces. These studies predate the current AI boom — which would only improve our ability to measure the problem and, at the same time, extrapolate it.

So watch the pillar land: the plantation becomes the algorithm. The overseer's variable judgment — a person who could be wrong, could be questioned — has become the algorithm's fixed, baked-in bias. The bias moved from a person to a system that hides behind math.

**[Q] "If the algorithm cannot see race, how does it reproduce racism?"**

**[Q] "Who is accountable when the code is wrong?"**

## P9 — Diaspora Sidebar: Brazil & Argentina

Now step back from the U.S. for a second, because this isn't only an American story.

Brazil's **Law of the Free Womb** (September 28, 1871) "freed" newborns — but bound them to their masters until age 21, a form of emancipation that the records themselves call a "new form of slavery." Private manumission — buying your freedom — became one of the only real doors out, and Brazil's Manumission Fund managed to liberate 20,000+ people. In Argentina, the *liberto* system conditioned freedom on military service — and then kept extending and re-enlisting it, while Afro-Argentine contributions were written out of the national memory.

**[Q] "Is the U.S. an exception or an example?"**

**[Q] "What does 'free' mean when freedom is conditioned?"**

The U.S. is one example of the pattern across the Diaspora: emancipation doesn't end coercion, it produces new ways of coercion. Same function, new legal form — outside U.S. borders too.

## P10 — Resistance, Memory & Erasure

So far this story has been about what was done to Black people. That's only half of it — because every single system on this board was met with organized resistance.

Harriet Tubman's Combahee River raid, July 1863, freed ~800 people in one night. The 1878 Fireburn in the Danish West Indies, where Queens Mary Thomas, Agnes, and Matilda led workers burning sugar plantations. The Haitian Revolution, 1791–1804 — the only one that ended in a free nation. Frederick Douglass's *North Star*, 1847: "Right is of no sex — Truth is of no color."

And memory is the front line now. Take **Tulsa, 1921**. On May 31st, a white mob attacked Greenwood — one of the most prosperous Black communities in all of America, so rich they called it "Black Wall Street." It started over a lie: a 19-year-old Black man, Dick Rowland, was accused of assaulting a white woman in an elevator — charges that were later dropped. That accusation was enough. Over ~18 hours, ~35 blocks were burned to the ground — ~1,200 homes and businesses destroyed, ~10,000 Black residents left homeless, and an estimated 100 to 300+ people murdered, though the official count was pinned at 36. Black residents were rounded up and detained by the National Guard — the victims treated as the suspects. Then came the second erasure: insurance claims denied, no one ever compensated, the city rebuilt over the rubble, and Tulsa's schools and records just… stopped mentioning it. It was buried out of the history books for *decades* — and investigators are still digging for unmarked mass graves today.

The MOVE bombing, 1985 — a home in Philadelphia bombed from the air by its own city government. Same pattern: the record gets cleaned up after the bodies get cleaned up.

The overseer could forget you; the algorithm cannot — unless someone audits it. That's why memory is the last front.

**[Q] "What does it mean that every system of control met organized resistance?"**

**[Q] "Whose memory is kept, and whose is erased?"**
